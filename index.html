“Usage limits apply”

Always run the best model your money actually buys.

API pricing is measured down to the millionth token. Subscription limits are usually described as “usage limits apply.” The benchmark below measures what’s actually behind that sentence — how much useful work each plan delivers against the same workload, compared with paying by the token.

This site is three layers. The ledger prices every subscription against that same work. Local prices the alternative — your own machine, no quota, a lower ceiling. Gate is the CLI that takes whichever one wins and points your editor at it.

What every column means, and what it does not mean
#
Position by AA proxy per dollar across every plan in view — all of them, because every plan now resolves to a figure — under the selected strategy. Read the rank together with the rung badge on the row: rank 1 on a measured allowance means “best, on the provider’s own published numbers”; rank 1 on a modelled one means “best if a plan at this price in this billing class behaves like the plans that do disclose”. Neither means “best plan to buy”.
Strategy
Two ways to spend the same allowance. Delivery picks, per plan, the configuration with the most AA Index per dollar of task cost — the most intelligence-weighted work per month. Planning picks the smartest configuration the plan grants and shows how many of those tasks the allowance covers. No configuration wins both; that trade-off is the point.
The five rungs
Every figure on the table says how it was obtained. The ladder runs strongest to weakest, and a figure built on another figure can never outrank the one underneath it:
  • measured the provider publishes the allowance in dollars. No assumption, no interval — eight plans, all of them metered.
  • observed a subscriber measured it and published the trace. Two plans, one account each; the interval is what the reports disagree about.
  • derived the provider publishes a countable rate of messages or requests, and that rate converts to less than the plan would ordinarily return — so the rate is the binding constraint and becomes the figure.
  • chained a ratio the provider prints itself (“5× Pro”, “12× Build”) applied to another plan’s figure. Warp publishes both halves, which is how the chaining is checked against its own arithmetic rather than against itself.
  • modelled nothing published and nobody measured it, so the allowance is fitted from the plans in the same billing class that do disclose. This is an estimate with a stated method and a stated spread — not a measurement, and never evidence that a plan delivers what the row shows.
Why billing class, and not one fit for everything
The plans that disclose split cleanly in two. Metered plans draw usage down against a credit balance and hand back roughly what you pay — Copilot, Warp, Zed and Replit sit between 0.5× and 2× the price. Flat plans charge a fee and impose rate limits, and the two anybody has measured hand back more than twenty times the price to whoever pushes them hardest. A single fit across both would average those into a multiple true of neither, so each class is fitted on its own anchors and every modelled row says which class it inherited.
The interval under every figure
Not decoration. A wide band is the cell’s main claim: the flat class rests on two reports, so its band is deliberately wide, and any row inheriting it carries that width down the chain. Where a figure is published exactly, the line says so instead of inventing a spread.
Best AA configuration · reasoning
Providers grant a family of models at several reasoning levels, and the levels differ enormously in cost per task. The strategy toggle picks which one this column shows: delivery picks the reasoning level with the most AA Index per dollar of task cost — rarely the smartest level; planning picks the smartest level the plan grants outright, whatever it costs per task. Two separate statements about what the plan is worth, neither one the best answer it can give you.
Price point, and “best plan at $X”
Filtering to a price point answers “of the plans costing about this much, which one delivers most per dollar”. Every bracket now has an answer, and most of those answers are modelled — the tile turns amber and says so whenever the leader rests on an estimate rather than a published figure. Highest intelligence is still not best value; it is only the one thing on this table that is measured directly rather than inferred.
AA Index · AA output / task · AA cost / task
Straight from the Artificial Analysis snapshot, per model configuration: its Intelligence Index, the answer + reasoning tokens it spent on one evaluated task, and what those tokens cost at that model’s published API rates. None of it is measured through the subscription. Where a plan grants a model the snapshot has not scored, the row shows the nearest scored model of the same family and marks the cell sibling model. The subline below the token count is AA Index per 10,000 generated tokens — a token efficiency measure specific to that model’s reasoning level.
Monthly capacity
Monthly allowance ÷ AA cost per task. It answers “how many AA-sized tasks would that allowance buy at API rates”, never “how many requests the provider will actually serve you” — their own credit accounting is different and not public. A published reset rate (“160 messages / 3h”) is treated as a hard ceiling on the month and caps the cell even when it is not what set the figure.
AA API-cost proxy / $
AA Index × monthly capacity ÷ plan price. Read it as intelligence potential per subscription dollar. The unit is meaningless on its own; only the comparison between two rows carries information, and only then when you have read both rungs. It ignores rate limits, harness overhead, context handling, retries and failed tasks — which is exactly the gap the rest of this page is about.
What is still missing
Nothing on this table is Layer 2. Not one figure comes from us running the plan and counting what it completed; the strongest evidence here is a provider’s own price list, and the second strongest is two strangers’ screenshots. Filling every cell removed the blanks, not the ignorance — it made the ignorance legible, and put a number on how much of the table it covers.

That is, in most cases, genuinely all of it.

You can compare API prices down to a fraction of a cent. For subscriptions used by millions of people every day, we often cannot answer how much compute the plan actually contains.

The wrong metric

A token is not what you’re buying.

What a subscription actually sells you is a bundle:

access to a specific set of models · an implicit pool of compute · rate-limit windows · a context budget · tools · caching behaviour · reasoning effort · a harness · sometimes web search, code execution or agents.

Two models can burn wildly different amounts of compute to succeed at exactly the same task. A model that is 20% cheaper per token and takes three attempts is not cheaper.

Cheapest tokens ≠ cheapest useful work.

The unit we propose

Measure work, not tokens.

Successful tasks / subscription

How many tasks complete correctly before a meaningful limit is hit.

Cost / successful task

subscription price ÷ successful tasks

PAYG-equivalent value

What the same runs would have cost at published API prices.

Break-even utilization

How much of the allowance you must actually use before the plan beats PAYG.

Failed tasks stay in the denominator of consumption and out of the numerator of value. A plan that lets you attempt more and finish fewer is not a better plan.

Subscription vs API

Where a subscription starts winning.

Under PAYG, task number 400 costs the same as task number 1. Under a subscription, every additional task inside the allowance drives the effective cost per task down — and every unused unit of allowance is money spent on nothing.

A subscription worth $2,000 of API compute is worth $0 of API compute if you use it twice a month.

Intelligence × efficiency

The smartest model is not necessarily the best deal.

Ranking one to ten hides the trade-off. A scatter doesn’t. Points on the frontier are the ones nothing else beats on both axes; points inside it are strictly worse than something else — more expensive and less capable.

Harness

You’re not just benchmarking a model.

Nobody buys “GPT-5.x”. They buy ChatGPT, or Codex, or Claude Code, or an IDE plugin — different harnesses wrapped around overlapping models.

The harness decides how many calls get made, how much context is resent, what gets cached, which tools fire, how retries are handled, and how often the task actually finishes. Those choices move token consumption by an order of magnitude and move success rate by double digits.

So the unit of comparison is plan + model + harness + workload — never a model alone.

Methodology

No proprietary magic score.

We keep intelligence, consumption and economics separate, and we publish the raw dimensions. There is no 0.43 × intelligence + 0.27 × speed + 0.30 × value here — that kind of formula hides the trade-off it claims to resolve.

The decision table uses one transparent Layer 1 proxy: theoretical AA-equivalent intelligence potential per subscription dollar. “Delivered” is reserved for our Layer 2 measurements. The proxy’s formula, raw dimensions and eligibility tiers stay visible. The Pareto frontier remains the cross-check: if the yield ranking repeatedly contradicts it, the ranking is wrong — not the frontier.

A composite score is a lens, not a verdict.

Every test follows the same loop

  1. Start from a fresh usage window.
  2. Run a standardized workload.
  3. Record model, harness, tokens where observable, cache usage, runtime, outcome, rate-limit consumption, errors and retries.
  4. Score the result: Pass / Partial / Fail. No three-decimal scores on a qualitative judgement.
  5. Repeat until the relevant subscription limit is reached.
  6. Compute the API-equivalent cost of the same runs where prices allow.

Two layers, never mixed

Layer 1 — Derived

Aggregated from third-party evaluations and published pricing and quota documentation. Broad coverage, cheap, fully sourced — and structurally unable to say “this plan completes N tasks”. It measures models over APIs, not subscriptions.

Layer 2 — Measured

Produced by our own runs against real subscriptions. Narrow, slow, expensive — and the only source of a claim about what a plan delivers.

Layer 1 makes the map. Layer 2 makes the claim.

Confidence, stated per cell

High published explicitly, in unambiguous units · Medium published in fuzzy units (“messages”, “credits”) requiring a documented conversion · Estimated derived from observation or partial documentation · Unknown not published, not observed — the cell stays empty.

We never invent a token quota a provider doesn’t publish. An empty cell is information; an unlabelled estimate is a lie. Every derived cell exposes its formula, its numeric assumptions, the source of the raw quota, the verification date and the confidence level.

Third-party model evaluations are an input, not ground truth: their methodological biases become ours, and a change of suite or version breaks historical comparability. Snapshots are versioned and kept, never overwritten.

Limitations

Subscription limits are messy. We don’t pretend otherwise.

Providers can change caps, time windows, which model you actually get, throttling policy, the amount of compute allocated, and the limits themselves during periods of high demand — all without announcement.

Which is why:

  • Every result is timestamped.
  • We report measured ranges when a single number would be misleading. Not “ChatGPT Plus = 183 million tokens”, but “under workload X, during test period Y, the plan completed 142–167 successful tasks before hitting its weekly constraint; equivalent API cost $84–$103.”
  • Snapshots older than 30 days are marked stale in the header. The freshness figure is the oldest verification in the table, not the newest.
  • Corrections are logged publicly. Nothing is silently overwritten.

Open risks, stated before launch

  • Terms of service. Deliberately consuming a plan to exhaustion for measurement may fall under anti-abuse or anti-automation clauses. Each provider’s terms are being read and our position documented before any Layer 2 run against them.
  • Cost. Layer 2 means paying for every plan tested, every month, indefinitely. That is why the MVP is three plans and not thirty.
  • Variance. A single run measures nothing. Minimum run counts are fixed per task and variance is published.
  • Non-determinism. Limits vary by hour, load, account age and region. A quota measured once is not a quota.
  • Grading. Pass / Partial / Fail is subjective on research and knowledge work. A sample is double-graded and the inter-rater agreement rate is published.

These are not disclaimers buried at the bottom. They are the reason the project exists.

Portfolio

Monthly budget:

    Illustrative allocation — not a recommendation. Prices are real; the coverage claim is what Layer 2 has to earn.

    The allocations above were written by hand. The ledger can do better than that — not by guessing coverage, but by refusing to let two plans on the same surface count twice.

    Why a naive optimizer is worse than none

    Two subscriptions covering the same workload do not add up — they substitute. A naive optimizer will always recommend stacking $20 plans. Stacking is not always naive — the measured flat plans return the same multiple of price at every tier up to $100 — but whether two $20 units of work equal one $40 unit of yours is exactly the substitution question only measurements can close. A real one has to model coverage per workload rather than the sum of allowances, substitutability between plans on the same workload, the non-zero cost of switching tools mid-task, and the fact that an unconsumed quota is worth exactly zero. The output is a coverage map, not a total of notional dollars.

    What’s actually behind these numbers

    Shipped — computed from data already in the repo
    • The global ranking, cross-checked against its own Pareto frontier — a yield ranking that contradicts it gets flagged, not trusted.
    • The local ceiling, and what running there costs you in intelligence against the smartest model a subscription grants.
    • Combinations across subscriptions — what the button above builds: one plan per vendor, one per harness, and headroom capped at what you say your month needs, so a second plan on an already-covered workload adds nothing.
    Not shipped — local hours and PAYG dollars in the same combination

    The builder above can’t add a local machine or a pay-as-you-go budget as a member of a combination, because nothing here prices them the way it prices a subscription. A local member needs a local dollars-per-task figure — energy plus amortized hardware — and neither lives in the data this site ships. A PAYG member needs a rate per model; the one rate this site publishes is a single blended figure used for one worked example on the ledger, not a price table you could plug a real model into.

    Tracked on the roadmap, alongside every other gap this site states rather than papers over.

    Why this exists

    We couldn’t find the answer, so we’re measuring it.

    There are dozens of sites comparing API token prices and model benchmark scores. We couldn’t find a reliable answer to a much simpler question:

    If I spend $20 on an AI subscription, how much useful work do I actually get?

    So we’re measuring it.

    In some workloads, subscriptions may deliver substantially more compute per dollar than equivalent API usage. The problem is that almost nobody measures it systematically. We are not going to tell you subscriptions are 20× cheaper than APIs — we don’t know that, and neither does anyone else.

    The ten that convert

    What a plan is worth, where the maths is allowed to run.

    Eight of the ten need no assumption at all: GitHub, Warp, Zed and Replit each publish the allowance in dollars, so the subscription price and the thing you are buying are quoted in the same unit. That is the entire trick, and all four of them resell API compute for a living. Not one plan that sells access to a model family does it.

    The other two are ceilings, not values

    ChatGPT Plus publishes a countable rate — 160 messages / 3h — and the Qwen Coding Plan publishes 90,000 requests / month. Both are convertible, but only into an upper bound. Drain every one of Plus’s eight windows a day, every day of an average month, and you would consume 38,960 tasks worth about $1,636 at API rates. Nobody is awake for 244 windows. Treat it as the ceiling of what the plan could be worth, and note that the ceiling is over 80× the price — which is why providers stop publishing numbers.

    A derived figure is only honest if you can move it yourself. Every ceiling above rests on exactly two numbers — the size of a task and the rate it is priced at:

    TASK = 12,000 input + 1,500 output tokens
    RATE = $2.00 / M in · $12.00 / M out

    cost per task  = 12,000 × $2/M + 1,500 × $12/M = $0.042
    tasks / month  = 160 × (24 ÷ 3) × 30.4375 = 38,960
    ceiling        = 38,960 × $0.042         = $1,636

    A mid-sized agentic turn, priced at mid-tier frontier rates. Halve the task size and the ceiling halves. 30.4375 is 365.25 ÷ 12, the average month, so a short month is not quietly billed as a long one. No other figure in the ledger is derived from anything.

    Reading the ledger

    What is missing, and why it stays missing.

    Filled is not known

    Every cell now carries a figure, and most carry one nobody published: fitted from the plans in the same billing class that do. The rung badge is the whole point — an unlabelled estimate would make the table look complete and make it worthless. A labelled one tells you exactly how far to trust the row.

    Prices move in weeks

    Google’s Ultra tier fell from $249.99 to $100 in May. Windsurf stopped existing — windsurf.com now redirects to Devin. Copilot switched from counting premium requests to quoting dollars. Every row carries the date it was checked.

    Half the vendors block the check

    OpenAI, xAI and Perplexity all refused automated requests to their own pricing pages during this pass. Those rows were priced from third-party trackers and are capped at estimated for that reason alone.

    The last column is the product

    “Measured” is hollow on all rows because measuring it means running real workloads against real subscriptions until the limits bite. That is the benchmark. This table is only the map of what nobody has measured yet.
